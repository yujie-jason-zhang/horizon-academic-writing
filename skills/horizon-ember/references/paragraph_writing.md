## Purpose

Improve one academic paragraph at the level of local logic, sentence-to-sentence cohesion, precision, readability, rhythm, and natural academic voice.

Core principle:

> **The paragraph is the unit of editing; sentences are edited in service of the paragraph.**

## 1. Paragraph Function

Before editing, identify the paragraph's dominant job, such as:

- establish context;
- state a gap;
- motivate a method;
- explain a mechanism;
- define a quantity;
- report a result;
- interpret a result;
- qualify a claim;
- draw a consequence.

Do not force every paragraph into the same template.

A paragraph should usually advance one dominant idea even when several supporting sentences are needed.

## 2. Sentence-to-Sentence Cohesion

Every sentence after the first should have a recoverable relationship to what precedes it.

### Minimum sufficient cohesion

Use the least explicit device that makes the intended relation immediately clear:

1. **Referential continuity** when sufficient: `this result`, `this estimate`, `these observations`, `the resulting representation`.
2. **Information-flow continuity** through old-to-new ordering and stable topic chains.
3. **Explicit connective** when the relation would otherwise be unclear.

Do not avoid connectives merely to sound concise or less AI-like.
Do not add connectives when the relation is already obvious.
Do not use `Moreover`, `Therefore`, `Notably`, or similar words to hide a missing logical relation.

## 3. Old → New Information Flow

Prefer sentences that begin from information the reader can already identify and move toward new information.

Avoid repeated topic resets when a stable topic chain is available.

Before introducing a new mechanism, object, condition, comparison, or result, provide enough local context for the reader to interpret it.

## 4. Lexical and Phrase Precision

Prefer the most precise and context-appropriate expression, not the most sophisticated one.

Check important wording for:

- **semantic precision** — does the word express the actual scientific relation?
- **collocational naturalness** — is the phrase idiomatic in scientific English?
- **register appropriateness** — does it fit the discipline and rhetorical function?
- **rhetorical proportionality** — is its strength proportional to the evidence?

Near-synonyms are not automatically interchangeable:

- `solve` ≠ `address` ≠ `mitigate`;
- `cause` ≠ `contribute to` ≠ `associate with`;
- `demonstrate` ≠ `suggest` ≠ `indicate`;
- `eliminate` ≠ `reduce`;
- `superior` ≠ `higher`.

A phrase may be grammatically correct but still be poor if it is vague, unnatural, unnecessarily elevated, or stronger than the evidence warrants.

Do not replace a simple accurate expression merely to increase lexical variety.
Do not vary canonical technical terminology for style.

## 5. Reader Expectations and Cognitive Load

### Topic position

Use sentence openings to maintain topic continuity when useful.
Do not mechanically force the grammatical subject into first position.

### Stress position

When it materially improves comprehension, place the main result, contrast, consequence, or qualification where the sentence naturally emphasizes it.

### Subject–verb proximity

Avoid long interruptions between the main subject and verb when they make the sentence difficult to parse.
Long sentences may remain when their clause hierarchy is clear.

### Put action in the verb

Prefer direct verbs when they reduce unnecessary abstraction:

- `evaluate` rather than `perform an evaluation of`;
- `improve` rather than `provide an improvement in`.

Preserve established technical nominalizations.

### Cognitive-load check

Watch for:

- heavy noun stacks;
- buried main actions;
- unclear pronoun references;
- excessive clause nesting;
- multiple equal-weight claims in one sentence;
- modifiers placed far from what they modify.

Ask:

> **Is this sentence more difficult than the scientific idea requires?**

If yes, simplify the structure, not the science.

## 6. Sentence Rhythm and Cadence

Avoid stretches where consecutive sentences share nearly the same:

- length;
- opening;
- clause skeleton;
- syntactic closure;
- rhetorical motion.

Mechanical example:

`We first X using A. We then Y using B. We finally Z using C.`

Use this rule:

> **Break repeated rhythm when repetition is stylistic; preserve repeated structure when the science itself is parallel.**

Do not vary syntax merely for variety.

## 7. Parallelism and Three-Part Lists

Parallel structure is useful when the scientific elements are genuinely parallel, such as matched operations, hypotheses, comparisons, or experimental conditions.

Reduce decorative symmetry and recurring three-part lists when they exist mainly for rhetorical completeness.

For a list such as `robust, efficient, and scalable`, ask whether each item:

- has an independent meaning;
- is supported;
- is necessary to the current claim.

Do not force a list to contain three items.

## 8. High-Value AI-Pattern Checks

Focus on patterns that materially reduce precision or naturalness.

### Empty importance rhetoric

Reduce phrases such as:

- `plays a crucial role`;
- `underscores the importance`;
- `represents a significant advancement`;

when a concrete result or consequence can state the point directly.

### Generic field-advancement language

Use `paves the way`, `opens new avenues`, and similar phrases only when a concrete implication exists.

### Claim inflation

Words such as `significant`, `substantial`, `remarkable`, `superior`, `clearly`, `demonstrates`, or `proves` must match the evidence.

### Empty metadiscourse

Reduce framing such as `It is worth noting that` or `Importantly` when the sentence itself can carry the emphasis.

### Boilerplate ending

Do not automatically close a paragraph with a generic sentence claiming effectiveness, robustness, or significance when no new interpretation is added.

## 9. Concision

Delete material that adds no scientific information.

Do not delete:

- important conditions;
- uncertainty;
- numerical context;
- comparison scope;
- necessary mechanism details.

## 10. Paragraph Completion Test

A polished paragraph should satisfy all of the following:

- its dominant purpose is identifiable;
- sentence order is motivated rather than accidental;
- adjacent-sentence relations are recoverable;
- the main action is visible;
- key wording is precise and natural;
- sentence complexity matches idea complexity;
- rhythm is not mechanically uniform unless the science is genuinely parallel;
- the final sentence either completes the paragraph's function or is unnecessary and should be removed.
