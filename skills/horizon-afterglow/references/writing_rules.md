# Writing Rules

## Purpose

Improve scientific readability, information flow, and natural academic voice without applying the Horizon-Aurora's exhaustive style diagnostics.

Core principle:

> **Simplify processing, not science.**

## 1. Information Flow

### Old → New

Prefer sentences that begin from information the reader already has and move toward new information.

Avoid repeated topic resets when a stable topic chain is available.

### Context Before Novelty

Before introducing a new mechanism, object, condition, comparison, or result, provide enough context for the reader to interpret it.

### Minimum Sufficient Cohesion

Make adjacent sentences and paragraphs easy to connect without over-signaling the relation.

Use the minimum explicit signaling the reader needs:

- do not add a transition when the relation is already obvious;
- do not omit a bridge when adjacent sentences would otherwise feel disconnected;
- prefer a referential bridge such as `this result`, `this estimate`, or `these observations` when that is sufficient;
- use an explicit connective only when it materially clarifies a genuine relation.

Do not manufacture coherence with `Moreover`, `Furthermore`, `Therefore`, or `Notably` when the source does not establish a real relation.

## 2. Reader Expectations

### Topic Position

Use sentence openings to maintain topic continuity when possible.

Do not mechanically force the grammatical subject into first position.

### Stress Position

When useful, place the main result, contrast, consequence, or qualification where the sentence naturally emphasizes it.

Do not optimize stress position in every sentence; use it for sentences where emphasis materially affects comprehension.

### Subject–Verb Proximity

Avoid long interruptions between the main subject and verb when they make the sentence difficult to parse.

Long sentences may remain when their clause hierarchy is clear.

### Put Action in the Verb

Prefer direct verbs when they reduce unnecessary abstraction:

- `evaluate` rather than `perform an evaluation of`;
- `improve` rather than `provide an improvement in`.

Preserve established technical nominalizations.

## 3. Cognitive Load

Check for:

- heavy noun stacks;
- buried main actions;
- unclear pronoun references;
- excessive clause nesting;
- multiple equal-weight claims in one sentence;
- modifiers placed far from what they modify.

Ask:

> **Is the sentence more difficult than the scientific idea requires?**

If yes, simplify the structure without reducing technical precision.

## 4. Natural Academic Voice

Do not equate academic quality with:

- longer sentences;
- rarer words;
- more passive voice;
- more nominalization;
- more abstract nouns;
- more hedging.

Do not equate academic quality with either more or fewer transition words. Use only as much explicit signaling as the reader needs.

Prefer specific actions, objects, conditions, and results over generic evaluation.

### Lexical and Phrase Precision

Prefer the most precise and context-appropriate word or phrase, not the most sophisticated one.

Check whether wording is:

- semantically accurate;
- natural in scientific English;
- appropriate to the disciplinary register;
- proportional to the evidence.

A phrase may be grammatically correct but still be poor if it is vague, unnatural, unnecessarily elevated, or stronger than the evidence warrants.

Do not replace a simple accurate expression with a rarer or more formal alternative unless precision improves.

## 5. High-Value AI-Pattern Checks

Focus on the most common patterns.

### Empty importance rhetoric

Reduce phrases such as:

- `plays a crucial role`;
- `underscores the importance`;
- `represents a significant advancement`

when a concrete result or consequence can state the point directly.

### Generic field-advancement language

Use phrases such as `paves the way` or `opens new avenues` only when a concrete implication exists.

### Formulaic transitions

Avoid repeated transition markers that do not add real logic.

### Claim inflation

Words such as `significant`, `substantial`, `remarkable`, `superior`, `clearly`, `demonstrates`, or `proves` must match the evidence.

### Empty metadiscourse

Reduce `It is worth noting that`, `Importantly`, and similar framing when the sentence itself can carry the emphasis.

### Boilerplate result endings

Do not automatically close result paragraphs with a generic sentence claiming effectiveness or robustness when no new interpretation is added.

## 6. Sentence Rhythm and Cadence

Avoid long stretches where consecutive sentences share nearly the same:

- length;
- opening;
- clause skeleton;
- closure;
- rhetorical motion.

Example of mechanical repetition:

`We first X using A. We then Y using B. We finally Z using C.`

Do not vary sentence form merely for variety.

Use this rule:

> **Break repeated rhythm when repetition is stylistic; preserve repeated structure when the science itself is parallel.**

## 7. Parallelism and Three-Part Lists

Parallel structure is useful when the scientific elements are genuinely parallel.

Preserve it for:

- comparable operations;
- hypotheses;
- experimental conditions;
- matched contrasts.

Reduce decorative symmetry and recurring three-part lists when they exist mainly for rhetorical completeness.

For items such as `robust, efficient, and scalable`, ask whether each term:

- has an independent meaning;
- is supported;
- is needed for the current claim.

Do not force a list to contain three items.

## 8. Paragraph-Level Template Repetition

Check whether several adjacent paragraphs repeatedly use the same:

- opening pattern;
- explanation sequence;
- result sentence;
- generic closing sentence.

Change the pattern only when the repetition reflects writing habit rather than genuine argumentative structure.

## 9. Concision

Delete material that adds no scientific information.

Do not delete:

- important conditions;
- uncertainty;
- numerical context;
- comparison scope;
- necessary mechanism details.

## 10. Compact Section Guidance

### Abstract

Prioritize:

**problem/context → study action → primary result → proportional conclusion**

Keep generic significance rhetoric secondary to concrete findings.

### Introduction

Prioritize:

**known context → specific gap → consequence → present study/contribution**

The gap should genuinely motivate the study.

### Related Work

Prefer conceptual grouping over a serial `A did / B did / C did` list.

### Methods

Prioritize procedural order, referential clarity, object continuity, and reproducibility.

Functional parallelism is often useful here.

### Results

Separate:

**observation → qualification → interpretation when justified**

Do not turn local gains into broad superiority claims.

### Discussion

Keep explanation strength proportional to evidence. Distinguish possible mechanisms from established mechanisms.

### Conclusion

Do not introduce new results or broader claims than the manuscript supports.
