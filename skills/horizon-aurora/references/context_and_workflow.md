# Context and Workflow

## Purpose

Provide enough manuscript-level context to support global polishing without treating every sentence as an isolated paraphrasing task.

## 1. Manuscript Context

Before polishing a major section or full manuscript, identify:

| Item | Question |
|---|---|
| Research problem | What problem is being addressed? |
| Central claim/purpose | What should the reader ultimately understand or accept? |
| Main contributions | What does the paper add? |
| Method/reasoning sequence | What dependencies or reasoning stages matter? |
| Section purposes | What job does each major section perform? |
| Major claim–evidence relations | What evidence supports the paper's major claims? |
| Canonical terminology | Which technical names must remain stable? |
| Notation/quantities | Which symbols, frames, values, or conditions need protection? |
| Local voice | Is the prose primarily direct, theoretical, engineering-oriented, restrained, or explanatory? |

Do not create an exhaustive sentence-level claim graph by default. Track major claims and expand only when the manuscript requires it.

## 2. Reading Depth

Use two levels:

1. **Global scan** — recover section structure, major claims, terminology, notation, evidence locations, and cross-section dependencies.
2. **Selective deep read** — inspect the active section and all context required to edit it safely.

Escalate beyond nearby context when:

- a claim recurs in distant sections;
- a term or symbol changes meaning;
- a numerical result is summarized elsewhere;
- an edit risks changing the scope of the manuscript's central claim.

## 3. Paragraph Context

For each important paragraph, ask:

1. What does the reader already know?
2. What is the paragraph mainly doing?
3. What new information must it establish?
4. What should the next reasoning step inherit from it?

Common functions include context, gap, motivation, mechanism, definition, comparison, result, interpretation, qualification, and transition.

Do not force every paragraph into the same template.

## 4. Scientific Claim Logic

For substantive claims, check whether the evidence and qualification are visible enough for the intended scope.

Distinguish:

- observation from interpretation;
- correlation from causality;
- benchmark-specific improvement from general superiority;
- empirical behavior from theoretical guarantee;
- plausible mechanism from demonstrated mechanism.

If a missing bridge requires new scientific content, expose the gap rather than inventing it.

## 5. One-Rewrite Principle

Use one main rewrite after diagnosis. Later passes verify and repair.

This reduces:

- terminology drift;
- claim inflation;
- broken topic chains;
- unnecessary reshuffling;
- unstable numerical phrasing;
- mixed stylistic voices.

## 6. Minimal-Change Preference

When several edits can solve the same problem, prefer:

1. delete redundancy;
2. adjust local order;
3. repair sentence structure;
4. split or merge sentences;
5. rewrite a sentence;
6. rewrite a paragraph only when local edits cannot restore clarity.

## 7. Source Problems

Separate:

- **source-to-output fidelity** — did polishing introduce an error?
- **source-internal inconsistency** — was the source already inconsistent?

Do not silently resolve source conflicts unless the author has made the intended correction clear.
